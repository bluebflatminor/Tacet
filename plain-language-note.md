# A Language You Can Read But Not Say

 *A short note on Tacet · Solbakken Research Initiative · CC0*

---

Here are two sentences:

> I saw the boat sink.
> I am told the boat sank.

They mean different things. The first one, you were there. The second, somebody
told you. In English we mark that difference with extra words. Plenty of languages
in the world mark it grammatically — you cannot make a statement in them without
declaring how you know it.

Tacet is a made-up language written in musical notation. It marks that same
difference, but it marks it in a place that sound cannot reach.

## The trick

On a piano keyboard, the black key between A and B has two names. Call it A-sharp
and you have named it going up. Call it B-flat and you have named it going down.
Same key. Same string. Same sound, exactly — not similar, identical.

Musicians have lived with this forever and it isn't a quirk. Which name you use
tells a reader where the music is headed. It is real information, written on the
page, and it vanishes completely the moment anyone plays it.

Tacet puts meaning there. Spell a word with sharps and you are saying *I saw this
happen.* Spell it with flats and you are saying *I heard about it.* Every sentence
must be spelled one way or the other. There is no neutral option.

So the two sentences at the top of this page look unmistakably different to anyone
who reads music. Play them, record them, and the two recordings are the same file.

## Why bother

There's a growing habit of measuring intelligence — human or machine — by what
comes out. Score the output, rank the score, declare a winner.

Tacet is a small argument against that habit, made as an object instead of an
essay. It's a language where the output provably doesn't contain the meaning. You
can listen to a Tacet sentence as many times as you like, with the best equipment
made, and you will not recover whether the speaker witnessed the event. Not because
it's difficult. Because it isn't there.

A test that only hears the performance will report that the two sentences are the
same sentence. It will be wrong, and nothing in the recording could ever tell it so.

## Where it came from

Music has quietly been used as language for a long time. Whistled languages in the
Canary Islands and Turkey carry full conversations across valleys. Talking drums in
West Africa reproduce the tone of speech well enough to send real messages. In the
1820s a Frenchman named Sudre built an entire language out of the seven notes of the
scale; it never caught on, but it worked.

All of those were made for the ear. Tacet is made for the eye, and that turns out to
change nearly everything about how it can be built — because a page can hold four
voices at once, and a mouth can only say one thing at a time.

## What isn't finished

Almost all of it. There are twenty words. Nobody has tried to read it. The most
basic test — render both sentences and check that the recordings really are
identical — has not been run, and if it fails, the whole idea fails with it.

That test is cheap and it comes first, which is the point. A thing that cannot fail
isn't an experiment. It's a decoration.
